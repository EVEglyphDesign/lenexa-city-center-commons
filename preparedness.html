In case you ever need it

Twenty quiet minutes now, on a normal afternoon, so that a bad night is a little less bad. None of this is urgent. None of it is required to use the Commons. Do one section and stop if that is what you have in you today.

Your phone already does this

Every phone sold in the last decade has an emergency call built into the hardware — no app, no unlock, no signal from us. It is faster than anything a web page can be, and it works when this site does not. Set it up. Use it first.

The Commons has a Reach help now button too, at the corner of every page, and it is a real option — one tap to your dialer with your cross-street already on screen. Both things are true. That is canon rule 19: we teach you the native tool, and we do not withhold ourselves as an option either.

1. Turn on your phone's own SOS

iPhone

  1. Open Settings › Emergency SOS.
  2. Turn on Call with Hold and Release. Then holding the side button and either volume button starts a countdown and calls 911.
  3. Optionally turn on Call with 5 Presses — pressing the side button five times fast does the same thing. Some people find this easier in a pocket.
  4. On that same screen, tap through to Emergency Contacts (it lives in the Health app's Medical ID). After an SOS call ends, iPhone texts those people your location unless you cancel.

Steps from Apple's iPhone User Guide and Apple Personal Safety User Guide. Menu names shift slightly between iOS versions — the words are close enough to find.

Android

  1. Open Settings › Safety & emergency › Emergency SOS.
  2. Tap Start setup. Confirm the emergency number is 911.
  3. Choose the trigger — on most Android phones, pressing the power button five times or more starts the emergency actions.
  4. Add emergency contacts so your location can be shared with them, and decide whether the phone should call automatically or ask you to confirm first.
  5. In Safety & emergency › Medical information, add anything a paramedic would want to know, and turn on Show when locked.

Steps from Google's Android Help and android.com. Samsung, Motorola, and OnePlus each move this menu around a little; search your Settings for “emergency” and you will land in the right place.

2. Test it without calling 911

You should never test an emergency feature by actually dialing. Two safe ways to find out whether yours works:

  • Turn on Airplane Mode first, then trigger the shortcut. You will see the countdown and the SOS screen appear; the call cannot connect. Cancel it, then turn Airplane Mode back off. You have now felt the muscle memory once, which is the whole point.
  • Say it out loud, on airplane mode: “Hey Siri, call 911” or “Hey Google, call 911”. Watch what your phone does. Cancel before it connects. Knowing whether your voice assistant answers at all — some are off by default, some need the screen on — is worth the two minutes.

If you accidentally place a real 911 call, do not hang up. Stay on and tell the dispatcher it was accidental. Hanging up sends officers to find out why.

4. If you are being watched

  • A phone can be monitored by someone who set it up or who knows the passcode. Hotline sites say this and they are not being dramatic.
  • A library computer or a friend's phone leaves no trace on yours. The Lenexa City Center Library is on this map and has public computers.
  • Texting works when talking is not safe. SafeLine Kansas takes texts at 847411 (send SAFE). The National DV Hotline has a browser chat.
  • This page loads nothing from us, stores nothing, and leaves no account behind. But your browser keeps history like any other page. Clear it if you need to.

5. Apps some people keep installed

Listed because they exist and people find them useful, not because we endorse them. Each is a company with its own terms, its own servers, and its own data practices — read them. None of them is us, and none of them is required.

  • Noonlight — hold a button, release it, enter a PIN; no PIN means dispatch is contacted.
  • bSafe — voice-activated alarm, live streaming to chosen contacts.
  • Silent Beacon — a physical bluetooth button paired to your phone.

What the Commons does and does not do

Does: put one red button on every page that opens your phone's dialer, a pre-written text to someone you trust, and the local non-emergency line — with your nearest cross-street on the screen so you can say where you are.

Does not: call anyone. Text anyone. Alert police. Notify the city. Record audio. Keep your location. Know that you tapped it. There is no server on the other end of this website, so there is nothing to notify and nothing to keep. See rule 18 and rule 3.
